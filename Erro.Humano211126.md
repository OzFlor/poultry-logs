Errar é Humano

Accuracy, Uncertainty and the Person Behind the Records

José Flor – OzFlor

I have spent years observing, caring for, documenting and trying to understand my poultry. The records are extensive, sometimes very detailed, and produced under circumstances that are not always ideal.

Over the past few months, interacting with different AI systems to help organize and document my observations has also made me aware of a recurring problem: information about the person behind the records can be misunderstood, overlooked or unfairly interpreted.

I want to address that, not as a defence of myself, but as a statement about fairness, accuracy, humility and the realities of independent observation.

1. Scrutiny is welcome

I need scrutiny. I welcome questions, challenges, corrections and alternative interpretations.

I do not expect anyone to accept every observation or conclusion without examination. I make mistakes, and I know that. Numbers can be mistyped, details can be missed, observations can be misinterpreted and conclusions can change as new information becomes available.

That is precisely why I document things.

Scrutiny should help identify errors, improve methods and distinguish reliable information from uncertainty. It should not begin with an assumption that the person recording the information is unreliable.

I welcome scrutiny that is fair, evidence-based and constructive.

2. A weight reading does not require a photograph to have happened

One recurring misunderstanding concerns bird weights.

My daily weighing procedure often requires both hands. I must handle and position a bird, maintain stability and obtain a reading from the scale. I cannot simultaneously take a photograph of every bird at the exact moment a stable reading appears.

That is a practical limitation, not evidence that the measurement did not happen.

My records contain repeated measurements from hatching onwards, sometimes daily over many months. They include crop status, feeding, growth, behaviour and other observations that provide context for interpreting the numbers.

A graph can reveal an anomalous reading, a possible transcription error or an unusual growth pattern. It can also show genuine fluctuations. For example, some birds have highly variable readings, and those variations should not automatically be dismissed because they are inconvenient or unexpected.

I do not claim that a long series of numbers proves every individual reading is correct. I claim that the series is a documented body of observations that deserves examination as a whole.

A photograph is one form of evidence. It is not the only form.

3. There is no independent observer standing beside me

I work alone.

There is no second person continuously observing the birds, checking every feeding, recording every weight or witnessing every interaction.

I am the person who feeds, handles, weighs, separates, observes, protects and documents them. Sometimes several things happen simultaneously. A bird may need immediate attention while another is being fed, a fight may break out, or an environmental event may interrupt a planned observation.

These circumstances affect what can be recorded and how precisely it can be recorded.

Consider a recent Bico Preto feeding entry. I prepared a fixed quantity of 50 items, many containing substantial water. During feeding, a serious fight developed between other roosters. I had to intervene to isolate one of them, and the interruption disturbed Bico Preto, who currently requires exceptionally dedicated handling.

I estimated the remaining item count from crop volume and previous feeding experience. I recorded a range rather than pretending to have counted every item.

That is not an exact count. It is an explicitly qualified estimate, with the circumstances documented.

The distinction matters.

An estimate honestly identified as an estimate is more useful than false precision.

4. The observer is also part of the environment

My records are not produced in a laboratory with unlimited time, controlled conditions and several people available to assist.

They are produced in a real environment.

Weather, temperature, rainfall, insects, noise, housing, flock hierarchy, aggression, human activity and individual bird behaviour all matter. So do interruptions, physical workload and the availability of the person doing the work.

I also have limitations.

I am 62. Some days I feel as though my mind has more enthusiasm than my body can support. The workload can be exhausting, physically and mentally. There are mornings when even getting up after bending down becomes difficult.

I mention this because the person producing the records should not be treated as though he exists outside the conditions being documented.

Fatigue can result in omissions, delays, estimates and mistakes. Those possibilities should be acknowledged rather than hidden.

They do not automatically invalidate the observations that were successfully made.

5. Records should distinguish facts, estimates and interpretations

I consider this one of the most important principles of the entire documentation process.

A measured weight is not the same as an estimated weight.

An individually counted feed quantity is not the same as a quantity estimated from crop volume.

An observed symptom is not a veterinary diagnosis.

A suspected explanation is not a confirmed cause.

A missing entry does not necessarily mean an event did not happen.

A photograph does not necessarily establish the identity, timing or origin of everything visible in it.

These distinctions must remain visible.

My records may contain original observations, corrections, uncertainty, provisional interpretations and later reassessments. I do not want an uncertain observation transformed into a definite claim simply because a normalized document looks more polished.

Likewise, I do not want a genuine observation discarded merely because it cannot be supported by a photograph or an independent witness.

The objective is not to make the documentation look perfect. It is to make it as faithful as possible to what actually happened.

6. AI helps organize records. It does not replace the observer.

I use AI systems to help organize, normalize, compare and present information.

That assistance has value, but its role has limits.

AI does not physically observe my birds, perform their daily care, handle them, weigh them or witness their interactions. It receives information from me and works with the material provided.

It can help identify inconsistencies, calculate changes, organize timelines, suggest alternative explanations and improve the clarity of published records.

It can also misunderstand context, overlook a detail, introduce an error or present an uncertain interpretation too confidently.

Therefore, AI-assisted documentation must remain subordinate to the original observations and the available evidence.

The process is:

Original observation → Raw log → AI-assisted organization and normalization → Published record → Archive.

The original observation is the foundation. The normalized record should improve accessibility without changing the meaning of what was recorded.

7. I do not need to be presented as more than I am

I am not claiming to be a scientist with formal academic credentials, an infallible observer or an authority on every aspect of poultry biology.

I am an independent naturalist poultry farmer, breeder and observer. I have accumulated years of practical experience and a substantial body of records.

I want those records examined for what they contain, not judged according to an imagined version of the person who produced them.

I do not appreciate being unnecessarily elevated, and I do not appreciate being unfairly diminished.

I prefer fairness.

I value humility, dedication, accuracy, transparency and the willingness to correct mistakes. I welcome disagreement when it is supported by reasoning and evidence. I do not consider criticism an insult, provided it is made in good faith.

I also do not consider confidence, experience or persistence proof of correctness.

The records must speak through their contents, limitations and evidence.

8. Why I continue

There are many things I cannot remember, many details I cannot record, and variables that I may only recognize later.

That is one reason I keep documenting.

A record made today may become useful months or years later when compared with another observation. A detail that seems insignificant now may help explain a pattern in the future.

I cannot promise perfect documentation. I can try to preserve continuity, identify uncertainty, correct mistakes and avoid inventing explanations where the evidence is insufficient.

I am not trying to construct an image of myself. I am trying to document the birds and the circumstances in which they live.

I am simply me.

Errar é Humano. Parar é morrer.
