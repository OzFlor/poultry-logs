Interpretation limits and question about the herbal tea

The stool record shows marked variation over the day: substantial visible blood in some watery stools, diffuse blood in some faecal material, smaller amounts in other observations, and intervals when no blood could be identified visually. Mucus and watery material were also present at several points.

The apparent reduction in blood in some stools is a useful observation, but it does not establish that intestinal bleeding has stopped. Conversely, the later dark stools in which blood could not be seen do not establish that blood was absent, especially where pea-flower coloration may interfere with visual assessment.

Did the herbal tea introduced at approximately 09:56 play a role in Gamma’s stool changes?

The current record cannot answer this. The stool abnormalities were already present before the tea was introduced, and the observations after 09:56 continued to vary. Timing alone does not establish a causal relationship. The tea’s effect, if any, remains unknown.

The white rubber-like structures observed at 08:19 also remain unidentified. Their appearance should not be treated as confirmation of intestinal tissue, and the stool descriptions alone cannot determine the underlying disease.

Given the repeated substantial visible blood after the recorded Baycox course, the record supports continued concern about intestinal haemorrhage. It does not establish whether this reflects ongoing coccidial disease, persistent intestinal injury, another condition, or more than one process. A veterinary examination and appropriate faecal or other testing would be needed to distinguish these possibilities.
