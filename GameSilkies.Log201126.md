3. Beta Phoenix — multiple-male mating and sperm competition

Recorded context

- 17 November: A Silkie rooster without a left spur was observed jumping off Beta's back. Beta was standing with her lower body on the surface, shaking her body and flapping her wings. This was recorded as an apparent mating event, but insemination was not confirmed.
- 19 November, approximately 16:50: The caretaker directly witnessed the Garden Table Silkie rooster successfully mounting Beta.
- 20 November: Beta was observed relaxed in a laying box with Gamma during the early morning. No further mating was recorded in this entry.

The identity of the rooster involved in the 17 November observation and its relationship to the Garden Table rooster must not be assumed. The possibility of another male mating with Beta remains relevant to breeding decisions.

How sperm from two roosters may interact

Hens store sperm in specialised sperm-storage tubules in the oviduct. Sperm from a mating can remain viable for days or weeks and be released gradually to fertilise successive eggs.

When a hen mates with two different roosters, the sperm populations can compete to fertilise eggs. They do not simply form two chronological batches in which the first male fathers the first eggs and the second male fathers the next ones.

Research on domestic chickens has demonstrated a last-male sperm precedence effect: sperm from the most recent male often fertilises a larger proportion of eggs. However, this is a statistical tendency, not a guarantee that the last rooster fathers every subsequent chick.

A 2024 study found that a second artificial insemination three days after the first could replace more than 70% of the previously stored sperm in the sperm-storage tubules under the study conditions. This demonstrates that later sperm can substantially alter the stored sperm population. It does not establish what happened inside Beta after natural mating.

A separate study using different rooster breeds, including Japanese Silkie, found that the most recent insemination fathered most chicks in the experimental groups. Chicks from different males could nevertheless appear during the same period.

Practical implications for Beta

1. If the Garden Table Silkie mated first and another rooster mates later: the later rooster may gain a fertilisation advantage, but the Silkie's sperm may still fertilise some eggs.
2. If the same rooster mates repeatedly: repeated mating may maintain or increase his representation in the stored sperm population, but does not prove exclusive paternity.
3. If Beta is separated now: this prevents further mating, but does not immediately remove sperm already stored from previous matings.
4. If Beta lays eggs over the following days: eggs from different dates may have different sires, and eggs laid close together may also have different sires.
5. If the objective is a controlled GameSilkies breeding record: the most reliable practical approach is to prevent further access by other males, record every possible mating event and laying date, and retain uncertainty about the sire of eggs produced during the sperm-storage period.

The 2018 study reported that offspring from the most recent insemination predominated, but offspring from earlier inseminations could still appear. Timing and the interval between inseminations influenced the proportions.

Conclusion for this record: Beta's mating history is now potentially mixed. A future egg cannot be assigned to the Garden Table Silkie rooster, Gamma or another male solely from its laying date. Phenotype alone is also insufficient to establish paternity reliably. Genetic parentage testing would be needed for a definitive result.

5. Interpretation and record limitations

- Weight changes are calculated from the reported current weights and 24-hour differences.
- Preliminary visual sex assessments of Ouro Preto, Arriba and Black are not confirmed.
- Blood observations are based on visual assessment and sleeping-location attribution; they are not laboratory-confirmed.
- The mating observations involving Beta differ in evidential strength. The 19 November event was directly witnessed; the 17 November event was recorded as an apparent mating.
- The identity of the male involved in each event must remain separate unless confirmed.
- Sperm competition research describes population-level tendencies. It cannot identify the sire of an individual egg without parentage evidence.
- The project record preserves observations and uncertainty rather than converting them into confirmed breeding outcomes.
