Reproductive management — Beta Phoenix

- 19:45: The caretaker requested that the normalization include an answer to a reproductive question concerning Beta Phoenix.

The Garden Table Silkie rooster may have successfully deposited sperm during mounting on 18 or 19 November. The question is what happens if another rooster successfully mates with Beta afterward, and how the sperm from two different roosters contributes to fertilization over subsequent days.

Scientific explanation: sequential mating and sperm competition in hens

Hens have specialized sperm-storage tubules (SSTs), principally at the uterovaginal junction of the oviduct. After mating, selected sperm enter these structures, where they can remain viable and subsequently be released for fertilization.

Consequently, mating with a second rooster does not automatically eliminate sperm from the first rooster.

The following principles are relevant:

1. Sperm storage: Sperm from both roosters may remain available in the reproductive tract. Fertile eggs can continue to be produced for approximately 2–3 weeks after a successful mating, although fertility generally declines with time.

2. Competition: Sperm from different males compete for access to storage and fertilization. The most recent male often has a fertilization advantage, but this is not an absolute rule.

3. Overlapping paternity: Eggs laid after successive matings may be fathered by different roosters. Even eggs laid close together can have different fathers.

4. Timing: Fertilization normally occurs shortly after ovulation. The timing of mating relative to oviposition and ovulation can influence which male fathers an egg.

5. Previous sperm remains relevant: A subsequent mating does not guarantee exclusive paternity by the latest rooster. Previously stored sperm can still fertilize later eggs.

A published 2018 experiment examined repeated inseminations using different rooster breeds, including Silkie White. It found a strong advantage for the subsequent insemination under the experimental conditions. In some groups, 82.4–100% of chicks were fathered by the subsequent male. However, these were controlled artificial insemination experiments, not a direct measurement of natural mating in Beta. These percentages should not be transferred directly to her situation.

Sources:

- "Timing and Interval Effects of Repeated Inseminations by Roosters on the Fathering of Chicks — 2018 research" (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6756406/)
- "Temporal dynamics of competitive fertilization in social groups of red junglefowl" (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7661449/)
- "Unique Physiological Mechanisms of Sperm Storage and Prolonged Sperm Survival in Hen Oviducts — 2025 review" (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12537510/)

Practical implications for Beta Phoenix

If the Garden Table Silkie rooster successfully mated with Beta on 18 or 19 November, and another rooster successfully mates with her on 20 November or afterward:

- Both males may contribute sperm to her reproductive tract.
- The later rooster may have a fertilization advantage.
- The earlier rooster may still father some eggs.
- Eggs laid over the following days and weeks cannot reliably be assigned to a particular rooster from mating dates alone.

Breeding-record recommendation:

Record every observed mating separately, including date, approximate time, male identity, observed mounting and whether cloacal contact or apparent sperm transfer was actually observed.

Do not classify a mating as confirmed sperm deposition merely because mounting was successful.

If Beta produces eggs after exposure to two males, label their parentage as potentially mixed / sire unconfirmed until genetic testing establishes paternity.

For GameSilkies breeding decisions, this distinction is important: a Silkie father cannot be assumed from the first observed mating, nor can a later rooster automatically be considered the exclusive sire.
