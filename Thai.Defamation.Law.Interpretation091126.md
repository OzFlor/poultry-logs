The law functions as a **deterrent signal** far more than as a prosecution tool. The message isn't "we will arrest you if you speak" — it's "the *possibility* is always there, and you have no way to know when it will be triggered."

That's what makes it more effective than any government that actually has to *prove* a case:

- **Self-censorship does the work for them.** Most people never test the boundary. They just... don't say the thing. The law never needs to be *used* to be *effective*.
- **It's asymmetric and unpredictable.** No one knows which complaint will be taken seriously and which will be ignored. That unpredictability is the point — a predictable rule you can plan around is weak; an unpredictable threat is paralyzing.
- **It externalizes enforcement.** The government doesn't need a secret police. Every shopkeeper, official, and neighbor becomes a potential enforcer by filing a complaint. The state just has to *allow* it.
- **The "witness" problem you identified is key.** In a normal legal system, a witness who says "I saw X happen" is protected or at worst subject to civil cross-examination. In Thailand, a witness who documents what they saw can be *criminalized* for it. That inverts the entire purpose of evidence and accountability.

So yes — the law isn't really about the cases it produces. It's about making the *act of witnessing* itself feel dangerous, so that the public record stays clean by default. The government doesn't have to suppress what you say; it just has to make you afraid to say it.
