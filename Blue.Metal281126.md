That observation—finding up to ~4 mm sharp-edged crushed rock ("blue metal" / basalt or granite aggregate) mixed into commercial feed pellets—is a serious quality-control failure, and it makes complete sense why you documented it.
1. What Likely Happened with the "Blue Metal" (~4 mm Crushed Stone)
In bulk Thai commodity handling, grains like corn, cassava chips, and broken rice are routinely dried outdoors on concrete or crushed-stone yards and scooped up with front-end loaders.
 * Cross-Contamination at the Mill: If a mill’s intake screens, magnets, and destoners are worn out, bypassed, or poorly maintained, ~4 mm gravel and crushed aggregate pass straight into the mixer and pellet die.
 * Intentional Weight Padding: Less scrupulous bulk suppliers and middlemen also sometimes adulterate bulk grains or mineral meals with crushed stone or sand to increase weight before selling by the kilogram.
 * Why It Matters for Smaller Birds: While adult birds use small, smooth grit in the gizzard to grind whole grains, sharp-edged ~4 mm aggregate in a pelleted ration (which is already ground and doesn't need coarse grit) poses a real risk of crop or gizzard impaction, internal mucosal abrasion, and displaced nutrient intake—especially in compact, lighter birds.
2. Why Cassava & Heavy Corn Signal a Low-Tier Formulation
 * Cassava (มันสำปะหลัง): Cassava root is one of the cheapest starch fillers in Thailand. It provides almost zero protein, negligible vitamins, and very low fat compared to grains, forcing the mill to rely heavier on cheap by-products or synthetic additives to hit the 17% protein floor.
 * High-Corn / "And/Or" Swapping: When corn makes up the bulk of the visible pellet matrix alongside cassava and defatted bran, the batch is built entirely around whatever commodity was cheapest that week rather than consistent amino-acid and micronutrient density.
3. Value of Your Photographic Documentation
Having timestamped images of the ~4 mm sharp aggregate inside the pellets alongside the feed bag batch details is solid empirical evidence. In commercial feed production, foreign mineral/stone contamination of that size violates basic Thai Department of Livestock Development (DLD) feed safety standards (พระราชบัญญัติควบคุมคุณภาพอาหารสัตว์)—even though retail bags hide behind broad "and/or" ingredient labels.
If you still have photos of those contaminated pellets or ever want to examine the texture and foreign particles in a new batch before feeding it, soaking a small sample of pellets in warm water in a shallow white dish makes any heavy stone, sand, or shell fragments immediately separate and sink to the bottom so you can inspect and measure them before they ever reach the flock.
